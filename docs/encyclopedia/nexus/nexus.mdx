In a company with multiple engineering teams, no single team owns everything. One team might focus on ordering, another builds the payment app, and another manages inventory.

But these applications still need to work together. Your team might build an order app that needs to charge a customer using the payment app another team owns. You don't need to know how payments work behind the scenes; you just need a way to use that functionality.

Nexus provides a foundation for building durable microservices. It gives Temporal applications a standard way to use functionality in other applications, while each team stays in control of its own code and logic.

## How does Nexus fit into an existing Temporal application?

Building blocks such as Activities, Workflows, Signals and Queries are execution-level primitives. They do their work inside one team's system. Nexus is a platform-level primitive: it shares durable operations across teams and Namespaces without exposing Workflow IDs, Task Queues or other internals.

Put simply, it's a durable communication layer for your application's infrastructure.

## Why use Temporal Nexus?

Nexus makes the connections between Temporal applications durable without requiring you to rebuild that infrastructure for every pair of teams. 

Let's say Payments, Fraud, and Shipping need to talk to each other constantly. Teams often connect them in different ways: custom gateways, HTTP clients, and webhooks, or Child Workflows and Activity wrappers with Temporal.

Each approach has gaps: Child Workflows require everyone in the same Namespace, Activity wrappers need per-target mTLS clients and boilerplate retry logic, and custom gateways aren't durable by default.

It can work but sometimes it can be like stringing tin cans between desks. It works until someone moves their desk.

The connections between applications are only as reliable as whatever you built yourself without Nexus, and the approach you decided to stitch together.

Nexus gives separate teams one shared way to connect. It extends the durability you already rely on to the space between teams. Each team stays in its own world, with its own code, security and release schedule, and shares only the specific things other teams can call. If something crashes halfway through, the work waits and picks up when the team is back.

## How many different ways can you connect with Nexus?

Every boundary between systems is a place where things can break. Nexus works across these:

- **Cross-Namespace:** Each team gets their own isolated environment with their own code, security, and infrastructure. Nexus connects them without either team having to expose their internals to the other.
- **Cross-Team:** Each team owns what they build and shares only the specific things they choose to make available to others. You expose only what you choose to share through a service contract.
- **Cross-Region (Temporal Cloud):** A team running infrastructure in one geographic region can call a team running in a completely different region all over a secure, encrypted connection managed by Temporal.
- **Cross-Cloud:** Temporal Cloud supports AWS and GCP, so your infrastructure choices don't limit how teams work together. A team on AWS can call a team on GCP.
- **Cross-Project (Temporal Cloud):** In Temporal Cloud, Namespaces are grouped into Projects. A caller Namespace in one Project can invoke a Nexus Endpoint owned by another Project, as long as it's on the Endpoint's allowlist.
- **Cross-SDK:** The team making the call and the team receiving it don't have to write code in the same programming language. One team can use Go, another Java, another Python. Nexus is available in the Go, Java, Python, TypeScript, .NET, and Rust SDKs.

The same reliability holds across all of them.

## How does Nexus work?

There are four main parts of Temporal Nexus:

- **[Service](/nexus/services):** A team's list of what a team offers to other teams. For example, a payments team offers a service to process payments.
- **[Operation](/nexus/operations):** A specific task or functionality another team can request. Some finish in seconds (sync, within a 10-second deadline). Others run for days, up to 60 days (async).
- **[Endpoint](/nexus/endpoints):** The name you use to reach another team's Service. You don't need to know where the team lives, how it built its system or what language it uses.
- **[Registry](/nexus/registry):** The Registry is where teams find available Endpoints. For example, a checkout team can find the existing payments service instead of building a new connection from scratch.

For example, the payments team creates a `PaymentService` that lists everything they offer to other teams such as saying "We offer: process a payment, issue a refund." in code. The checkout team calls `processPayment` Operation. It might complete in milliseconds (sync) or take days waiting for bank confirmation (async, up to 60 days).

The checkout team reaches the payments team through an Endpoint named something like `payments-endpoint`. The checkout team just uses the name. They don't know what Namespace the payments team uses, what language they wrote it in, or how their Workers are set up. The Registry is a central directory where all Endpoints are listed.

The checkout team finds `payments-endpoint` in the Registry, calls it by name (Endpoint), and requests `processPayment` (Operation), which belongs to `PaymentService` (Service), without building a custom connection from scratch.

## Caller vs handler

In Temporal Nexus, the caller is the application or Workflow that wants to use a capability, and the handler is the code on the other team's Worker that provides that capability. The caller starts a Nexus Operation through an Endpoint with the Service defining what's available.

The caller doesn't need to know:

- Which Namespace the handler runs in.
- Which Task Queue it uses.
- What language the handler team wrote its code in.
- Whether the handler starts a Workflow or an Activity.
- How the underlying implementation works.

The handler is the implementation registered with the other team's Worker. It receives the request, decides what to do with it, and returns the result. The handler team can change its implementation completely, for example, swap a Workflow for a Standalone Activity or rewrite the implementation in another language, without the caller needing to know, as long as the contract stays the same.

The caller knows the contract. The handler owns the implementation. Nexus keeps them cleanly separated so both teams can evolve independently.

Underneath, Nexus handles automatic retries and at-least-once execution across every team, region and cloud. Workflows or clients call Nexus Operations, Nexus routes the request to the right team's Worker through an Endpoint, and Temporal handles the execution - whether that's a Workflow or a Standalone Activity.

## Nexus as durable microservices

Nexus gives you durable microservices. It extends Temporal's durability to the connections between applications, not just within them.

Each team owns a service with a clear contract, runs it in its own Namespace, and lets other teams call it through an Endpoint.

Giving each team its own Namespace improves modularity, security, debugging and fault isolation. The difference from ordinary microservices is what happens when a call goes wrong. A normal network call is something the caller has to make reliable on its own, while Nexus gives the caller a durable handoff.

If the other team's service is down, the request waits and runs when it's back. And a call isn't limited to a quick request and response. Nexus supports Operations that go far beyond a traditional RPC where async Operations can run for up to 60 days, while sync Operations complete within seconds. Nexus brings microservice-style modularity to Temporal where each team owns its Namespace and exposes a service contract, but unlike ordinary microservices, cross-team calls are durable by default.
